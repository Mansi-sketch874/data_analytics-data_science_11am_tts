# What Is Agile Methodology?

Agile methodology is an iterative and incremental approach to managing and delivering products, software, services, and projects. Work is divided into small, valuable pieces and delivered regularly. Teams use feedback from customers and stakeholders to improve the product throughout the project.

Agile does not mean working without a plan. It means creating a flexible plan, delivering in small steps, learning from results, and adjusting priorities when requirements or business conditions change.

## Why Agile Is Used

Traditional projects may define most requirements at the beginning and deliver the complete product at the end. Agile delivers usable results frequently, which helps a team:

1. Get customer feedback early.
2. Find technical or business problems sooner.
3. Respond to changing requirements.
4. Reduce the risk of building the wrong product.
5. Deliver business value in small increments.
6. Improve quality through regular testing and review.
7. Make progress visible to stakeholders.

## The Agile Manifesto

The Agile Manifesto is based on four values:

### 1. Individuals and Interactions Over Processes and Tools

Tools and processes are useful, but communication, collaboration, and skilled people are more important.

### 2. Working Software Over Comprehensive Documentation

Documentation has value, but a working product gives stronger evidence of progress. Agile does not mean that documentation is unnecessary.

### 3. Customer Collaboration Over Contract Negotiation

Regular collaboration helps the team understand real needs and deliver a more useful result.

### 4. Responding to Change Over Following a Plan

A plan is important, but the team should change direction when new information makes the original plan less valuable.

## The Twelve Agile Principles

1. Satisfy customers through early and continuous delivery of valuable work.
2. Welcome changing requirements, even late in development.
3. Deliver working products frequently.
4. Encourage close daily collaboration between business and technical people.
5. Build projects around motivated individuals and give them support and trust.
6. Prefer direct communication because it is efficient and clear.
7. Use working software or a working product as the primary measure of progress.
8. Maintain a sustainable pace that can continue over time.
9. Pay continuous attention to technical excellence and good design.
10. Keep work simple and avoid unnecessary effort.
11. Let self-organizing teams create the best solutions.
12. Reflect regularly and adjust the process to become more effective.

## Important Agile Terms

### Agile Team

An Agile team is a group of people with the skills needed to plan, build, test, deliver, and improve a product. The team collaborates closely and shares responsibility for outcomes.

### Stakeholder

A stakeholder is a person or group affected by the product or interested in its results. Examples include customers, business leaders, users, operations teams, compliance teams, and support teams.

### Customer or Product User

The customer or user receives value from the product. Their needs, feedback, and problems help the team decide what to build.

### Product Owner

The Product Owner represents product and customer interests. Their responsibilities commonly include:

- defining the product goal;
- ordering the product backlog;
- clarifying requirements and acceptance criteria;
- communicating with stakeholders;
- accepting or rejecting completed work based on agreed criteria;
- maximizing the value delivered by the team.

The Product Owner does not work alone. They collaborate with developers, users, and stakeholders.

### Scrum Master

The Scrum Master helps the team understand and use Scrum. They facilitate important events, remove or help resolve impediments, coach the team, and encourage continuous improvement. A Scrum Master is not simply a project manager who assigns every task.

### Developers

Developers are the people who create the product increment. In Scrum, the term includes all specialists needed to produce a usable result, such as programmers, testers, analysts, designers, data engineers, and data scientists.

### Cross-Functional Team

A cross-functional team contains the skills required to complete valuable work without depending on a separate team for every step.

### Self-Managing Team

A self-managing team decides how to organize its work and who performs each task. It remains accountable for meeting its goals and quality standards.

## Agile Planning Terms

### Product Vision

The product vision describes the future the product is intended to create and the problem it will solve.

### Product Goal

The product goal is a clear objective that guides the team toward a valuable future state.

### Roadmap

A roadmap is a high-level view of planned outcomes, themes, releases, or milestones. It should communicate direction without pretending that every future detail is fixed.

### Backlog

A backlog is an ordered list of work, ideas, improvements, defects, research, and technical tasks. The most valuable or urgent items are usually placed near the top.

### Product Backlog

The product backlog contains potential work for the entire product. It changes as the team learns more about users, technology, and business priorities.

### Sprint Backlog

The sprint backlog contains the selected work for a sprint and the plan for delivering it. The team updates it as it learns more during the sprint.

### User Story

A user story is a short description of a need from a user's point of view.

Common format:

```text
As a [type of user],
I want [a capability],
so that [a benefit].
```

Example:

```text
As a sales manager,
I want to filter the dashboard by region,
so that I can compare regional performance.
```

### Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria are the conditions that must be true for a user story to be accepted. They make expectations testable and reduce misunderstanding.

Example:

- The user can select one region from a filter.
- All dashboard charts update to the selected region.
- The dashboard shows a message when no records match.

### Epic

An epic is a large body of work that is too broad for one iteration. It can be divided into smaller features or user stories.

### Feature

A feature is a product capability that provides value to users. A feature may contain several user stories.

### Task

A task is a smaller technical or practical activity required to complete a user story, such as writing a query, designing a chart, or testing a calculation.

### Bug or Defect

A bug is a behavior that does not meet the expected requirement or quality standard. Bugs should be recorded, prioritized, fixed, and tested.

### Technical Debt

Technical debt is the future cost created by choosing a quick or imperfect technical solution. Some technical debt is intentional, but it should be documented and managed rather than ignored.

### Spike

A spike is a time-boxed research or investigation task used to reduce uncertainty. For example, a team may run a spike to test whether an API can provide the required data.

## Estimation Terms

### Story Points

Story points are relative units used to estimate the effort, complexity, risk, and uncertainty of a backlog item. They are not a direct measurement of hours.

### Planning Poker

Planning poker is a collaborative estimation technique. Team members privately choose estimates, reveal them together, explain differences, and agree on a shared estimate.

### Velocity

Velocity is the amount of estimated work a team completes during an iteration. It can help with forecasting when used carefully, but it should not be used to compare teams or pressure people to inflate estimates.

### Capacity

Capacity is the actual amount of time and availability the team has for work during an iteration. Holidays, support duties, meetings, and other responsibilities affect capacity.

### Timebox

A timebox is a fixed maximum duration for an activity. A timebox encourages focus and prevents discussions or tasks from expanding indefinitely.

### Definition of Ready

The Definition of Ready is a team's shared checklist for deciding whether a work item is sufficiently understood to begin. It may include a clear objective, acceptance criteria, dependencies, and available data.

### Definition of Done

The Definition of Done is the shared quality standard for completed work. It may require implementation, testing, documentation, security review, deployment, and stakeholder acceptance.

## Scrum Framework

Scrum is a lightweight Agile framework for managing complex work. It uses defined accountabilities, events, and artifacts. Scrum is one way to apply Agile; Agile and Scrum are not exactly the same thing.

### Sprint

A sprint is a fixed-length iteration in which a team creates a usable product increment. Sprints commonly last one to four weeks. The team aims to achieve a Sprint Goal and meet the Definition of Done.

### Sprint Planning

Sprint Planning starts a sprint. The team discusses:

1. Why is this sprint valuable?
2. What work can be completed?
3. How will the selected work be delivered?

The result is a Sprint Goal and a Sprint Backlog.

### Daily Scrum

The Daily Scrum is a short daily event for Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan. It is not meant to be a long status report to a manager.

### Sprint Review

During the Sprint Review, the team and stakeholders inspect the result of the sprint, discuss feedback, and consider what to do next. It is a working session, not only a presentation.

### Sprint Retrospective

The Sprint Retrospective is a team reflection on people, collaboration, tools, quality, and process. The team selects practical improvements for future work.

### Product Increment

An increment is a usable, verified addition to the product. Several increments can be created during one sprint, and an increment may be released when it meets the required quality standard.

## Kanban Terms

Kanban is an Agile way of managing and improving the flow of work. It does not require fixed-length sprints.

- **Kanban board:** A visual board showing work stages such as To Do, In Progress, Review, and Done.
- **Work item:** A unit of work represented on the board.
- **Work in progress (WIP):** Work that has started but is not finished.
- **WIP limit:** A maximum number of items allowed in a stage. It reduces multitasking and exposes bottlenecks.
- **Pull system:** Team members pull new work when they have capacity instead of receiving too many items at once.
- **Cycle time:** Time from starting an item to completing it.
- **Lead time:** Time from requesting an item to delivering it.
- **Throughput:** Number of work items completed in a period.
- **Flow efficiency:** The proportion of total time that an item is actively being worked on.

## Other Agile Frameworks and Practices

### Extreme Programming (XP)

XP emphasizes engineering practices such as test-driven development, pair programming, continuous integration, simple design, and frequent releases.

### Lean

Lean focuses on maximizing customer value and reducing waste, delays, unnecessary features, defects, and handoffs.

### Crystal

Crystal is a family of lightweight methods that adapts practices to the size of the team and the criticality of the work.

### Feature-Driven Development

Feature-Driven Development organizes work around small, client-valued features and frequent progress.

### Scaled Agile

Scaled Agile approaches coordinate multiple teams working on a large product or organization. Scaling adds coordination needs; it does not remove the need for clear goals, feedback, and working increments.

### User-Centered Design

User-centered design studies user needs and behavior and includes users in design and validation activities. It often works alongside Agile development.

### Continuous Integration

Continuous integration means regularly merging changes into a shared codebase and automatically checking them with tests and quality tools.

### Continuous Delivery

Continuous delivery keeps the product in a releasable state through automation, testing, and deployment practices.

### Refactoring

Refactoring improves the internal structure of code without changing its external behavior. It helps control technical debt.

## Agile Metrics

Metrics should support learning and improvement, not punish teams.

- **Velocity:** Estimated work completed per iteration.
- **Burndown chart:** Remaining work over time.
- **Burnup chart:** Completed work compared with total scope over time.
- **Cumulative flow diagram:** Work items distributed across workflow stages.
- **Cycle time:** Time taken to complete started work.
- **Lead time:** Time from request to delivery.
- **Throughput:** Completed work items per period.
- **Defect rate:** Number or proportion of defects found.
- **Escaped defects:** Defects discovered after release.
- **Release frequency:** How often usable changes are released.
- **Customer satisfaction:** Feedback showing whether the product solves the user's problem.

No single metric describes team health. Metrics should be considered together with quality, customer value, team sustainability, and context.

## Agile Process Example

Suppose a company wants a sales dashboard.

1. Stakeholders explain the business problem and desired decisions.
2. The Product Owner creates an ordered backlog.
3. The team breaks the dashboard into stories for data connection, cleaning, KPIs, filters, charts, and validation.
4. During Sprint Planning, the team selects a realistic set of stories.
5. The team develops, tests, and reviews the dashboard during the sprint.
6. The team demonstrates the usable increment at the Sprint Review.
7. Stakeholders provide feedback, such as adding a region filter or changing a KPI definition.
8. The team discusses its process in the Retrospective.
9. The Product Owner reorders the backlog based on value and feedback.
10. The next sprint delivers another improvement.

## Agile in Data Analytics

Agile is useful in data analytics because requirements, data quality, and business questions often change as analysis progresses.

An analytics team may use Agile to:

- deliver a first version of a dashboard with a few trusted KPIs;
- validate metric definitions with business users;
- investigate data quality through spikes;
- develop reusable data pipelines incrementally;
- test a forecasting method on a small sample before scaling it;
- review insights frequently with decision-makers;
- prioritize work according to business impact and data availability.

Example analytics backlog items:

- As a sales manager, I want monthly revenue by region so that I can identify underperforming areas.
- As a marketing manager, I want campaign conversion rates so that I can allocate budget effectively.
- As a data engineer, I want automated checks for missing order IDs so that reporting remains reliable.
- As a finance analyst, I want budget-versus-actual variance so that leaders can investigate overspending.

For analytics work, the Definition of Done may include validated source data, documented calculations, tested SQL or Python code, reviewed visualizations, clear assumptions, and an approved dashboard or report.

## Benefits of Agile

- Faster feedback and earlier value.
- Better visibility into progress and risks.
- Greater ability to respond to change.
- Frequent opportunities to improve quality.
- Stronger collaboration between technical and business teams.
- Lower risk of delivering an unwanted product.

## Limitations and Common Risks

- Poorly defined priorities can create constant disruption.
- Too many meetings can reduce productive work.
- Without a clear product goal, the backlog becomes a list of unrelated requests.
- Teams may focus on velocity instead of customer value.
- Frequent changes can create technical debt if quality is ignored.
- Agile cannot solve missing skills, unavailable stakeholders, poor data, or unrealistic deadlines by itself.
- Scaling Agile with excessive processes can make work slower and less flexible.

## Agile Best Practices

1. Start with a clear product goal and measurable outcomes.
2. Keep the team cross-functional and small enough to communicate effectively.
3. Prioritize work by customer value, risk, urgency, and learning.
4. Define acceptance criteria before development begins.
5. Deliver a small usable increment instead of many unfinished tasks.
6. Protect quality through testing, review, documentation, and automation.
7. Invite real users and stakeholders to reviews.
8. Limit work in progress and finish started work before beginning too much new work.
9. Treat retrospectives as opportunities for concrete action.
10. Use metrics for learning rather than individual punishment.

## Agile and Waterfall Comparison

| Area | Agile | Waterfall |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | Continuous and adaptive | Mostly completed at the beginning |
| Delivery | Small increments | Usually one major delivery |
| Requirements | Can evolve through feedback | Usually defined early |
| Testing | Continuous throughout work | Often concentrated later |
| Customer involvement | Frequent | Often concentrated at milestones |
| Response to change | Expected and managed | More expensive after approval |
| Best fit | Uncertain or changing work | Stable and highly predictable work |

Neither approach is automatically best for every project. The choice should depend on risk, regulation, requirement stability, team structure, and the cost of change.

## Conclusion

Agile is a mindset and collection of practices for delivering value through short feedback cycles, collaboration, transparency, and continuous improvement. Scrum and Kanban are common ways to organize Agile work. A successful Agile team has a clear goal, an ordered backlog, usable increments, shared quality standards, regular feedback, and the ability to improve its process.

## Short Answer

Agile methodology is an iterative approach in which teams deliver small, valuable increments, receive feedback, and adapt their plans. Important Agile terms include Product Owner, Scrum Master, sprint, backlog, user story, acceptance criteria, Definition of Done, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Retrospective, Kanban, WIP limit, velocity, and continuous improvement.
